This chapter introduces the concepts on which the remainder of the thesis rests, in the
order in which they were named in Chapter~\ref{kap:Einleitung}. It first describes the
device that produces the data -- the electric linear actuator and its wear and failure
mechanisms (Section~\ref{sec:Grundlagen_Aktuator}). It then defines what is meant by
\emph{data quality} and why it is a prerequisite for any later data-driven use of the
recordings (Section~\ref{sec:Grundlagen_Datenqualitaet}). Section~\ref{sec:Grundlagen_TS}
covers the time-series preprocessing methods on which the quality checks of this work are
built: cycle detection, standstill detection, and the robust derivation of thresholds from
observed data distributions. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:Grundlagen_Pipeline} summarises the
existing preprocessing pipeline that turns the raw recordings into structured motion
cycles and thereby forms the starting basis of this thesis.

\section{Electric Linear Actuators}
\label{sec:Grundlagen_Aktuator}

\subsection{Structure and Operating Principle}
\label{subsec:Grundlagen_Aufbau}

An \emph{actuator} is a component that converts an input energy form into a controlled
mechanical motion. An \emph{electric linear actuator} converts the rotation of an electric
motor into a linear (straight-line) movement of an output rod \parencite{TODO-actuator-fundamentals}.
In the rod-style actuators considered here, this conversion is typically realised by a
\emph{ball screw} drive: the motor turns a threaded spindle, and a nut running on
recirculating balls translates the rotation into a low-friction axial motion of the rod,
which is guided by a linear bearing \parencite{ma_wear_2024}. The actuator relevant to
this work is \emph{position-controlled}: a control loop drives the rod to follow a
prescribed \emph{motion trajectory} rather than simply being switched on and off.

The trajectory used during an endurance test is, at its core, an ``out-and-back''
movement between a \emph{rest position} and a defined target position, the \emph{full
stroke}. One such out-and-back movement is a \emph{motion cycle} and is the central
analytical unit of this thesis: all quality checks and visualizations refer to individual
cycles rather than to arbitrary time windows. Because the actuator is position-controlled,
the position signal is the natural reference for delimiting a cycle -- a cycle begins when
the position leaves the rest position and ends when it returns to it. During each cycle,
several physical quantities are recorded in parallel by separate sensors, most importantly
position, velocity, current, pressure, temperature, and three-axis vibration; these
signals differ substantially in their native sampling rate, which is relevant for their
later processing (Section~\ref{sec:Grundlagen_TS}).

\subsection{Types and Fields of Application}
\label{subsec:Grundlagen_Typen}

Electric linear actuators are used wherever a defined, repeatable linear force or
positioning movement is required, for example in valve and flow control, industrial
automation, and process equipment \parencite{TODO-actuator-fundamentals}. Compared with
hydraulic or pneumatic drives, electric actuators offer precise position control, clean
operation without a pressure supply, and straightforward integration into digital control
and monitoring systems -- which is also what makes their internal signals available for
the kind of data-driven condition monitoring targeted in this work. Because such actuators
are frequently deployed in demanding or safety-relevant industrial applications, their
service life and reliability must be demonstrated before deployment, which is the purpose
of the endurance tests described next.

\subsection{Loads, Wear, and Failure Mechanisms}
\label{subsec:Grundlagen_Verschleiss}

During operation, an electric linear actuator is subject to mechanical loads that lead to
gradual wear of its moving parts. In a ball-screw drive, the dominant wear mechanisms
affect the contact surfaces of the screw, nut, and balls as well as the linear guide;
this wear accumulates slowly over a very large number of cycles and manifests as a gradual
change in the drive's behaviour, such as increased friction, altered current draw, or
changed motion characteristics \parencite{ma_wear_2024}. Because such degradation is slow
and cumulative, it can only be observed reliably over long operating times rather than in
a short trial.

This is the rationale for the \emph{endurance test}: to expose the slow wear and ageing of
a design under realistic, sustained load, a device under test is operated continuously for
months while it repeats the same motion cycle, and its sensor signals are recorded
throughout \parencite{oconnor_practical_2012}. Life and accelerated-life testing of
electromechanical actuators is an established method for uncovering the weak points of a
design and for estimating its service life before the product reaches the
field \parencite{han_accelerated_2019}. The long duration and high sampling rate of these
tests are precisely what make the resulting recordings large and heterogeneous -- and
therefore what make a systematic preprocessing and quality-assurance step necessary before
the data can be used.

\section{Data Quality}
\label{sec:Grundlagen_Datenqualitaet}

\subsection{The Concept and Dimensions of Data Quality}
\label{subsec:Grundlagen_DQ_Dimensionen}

\emph{Data quality} is commonly defined as the degree to which data is fit for its
intended use rather than as an absolute, use-independent property of the
data \parencite{TODO-data-quality}. This ``fitness for use'' view is usually operationalised
through a set of \emph{quality dimensions}. Widely cited dimensions
include \emph{completeness} (are all required values present?), \emph{consistency} (are the
values free of internal contradictions?), \emph{accuracy} (do the values reflect the real
quantity they represent?), \emph{validity} or \emph{plausibility} (do the values lie in a
physically possible range?), and \emph{timeliness} \parencite{TODO-data-quality-dimensions}.
The same idea is codified in the international standard for a data-quality model, which
organises quality into a comparable set of characteristics
\parencite{TODO-iso25012}. For the recordings considered here, the most relevant dimensions
are completeness (are all required sensor signals present in a cycle, with enough
samples?), plausibility (did the actuator actually perform a full, physically meaningful
stroke?), and consistency (are the timestamps ordered and free of large gaps?). These
dimensions provide the vocabulary for the quality criteria developed in
Chapter~\ref{kap:Methodik}.

\subsection{Data Quality versus Signal Health}
\label{subsec:Grundlagen_DQ_Gesundheit}

An important conceptual distinction for this work is that between \emph{data quality} and
\emph{signal health}. A cycle can be of high technical data quality -- complete, correctly
sampled, with valid values -- and yet exhibit physically unusual behaviour that reflects
genuine wear or an incipient fault of the actuator. Conversely, a cycle can be technically
unusable (for example because a sensor was frozen) without saying anything about the health
of the machine. Data-quality preprocessing and the later detection of degradation must
therefore remain separate steps: the goal of preprocessing is to remove cycles that are
\emph{bad data}, while deliberately retaining cycles that are merely \emph{unusual but
real}, because the latter are exactly the cases a downstream condition-monitoring model is
meant to learn from \parencite{TODO-condition-monitoring}. Confusing the two -- discarding
a genuinely degraded cycle as an ``outlier'' -- would remove the very signal that later
analysis depends on. Keeping this distinction explicit is one of the reasons the quality
criteria in this thesis are designed to be conservative and physically justified rather
than purely statistical.

\subsection{Data Quality as a Prerequisite for Data-Driven Analysis}
\label{subsec:Grundlagen_DQ_Voraussetzung}

The practical importance of data quality follows from a simple observation: the output of a
data-driven analysis can be no better than the data it is trained on, a principle often
summarised as ``garbage in, garbage out'' \parencite{TODO-data-quality}. In the context of
prognostics and health management, the curation of a clean, well-characterised dataset is
consistently identified as a foundational step that precedes and constrains any modelling
effort \parencite{TODO-phm}. Undetected artefacts -- frozen sensors, incomplete cycles, or
recording gaps -- would be silently learned by a model as if they were real machine
behaviour, degrading its ability to distinguish genuine wear from measurement problems.
Establishing and documenting data quality before modelling is therefore not an optional
refinement but a prerequisite, and it is the specific contribution this thesis makes to the
overall data-processing chain.

\section{Time-Series Preprocessing of Sensor Data}
\label{sec:Grundlagen_TS}

\subsection{Sensor Time Series and Heterogeneous Sampling}
\label{subsec:Grundlagen_TS_Abtastung}

Each recorded sensor signal is a \emph{time series}: a sequence of measured values, each
associated with a timestamp \parencite{aigner_visualization_2023}. The signals in this work
are discrete and only approximately periodic -- the interval between successive samples
varies slightly around a nominal value -- so small timestamp variations are expected and
should not be treated as errors. More importantly, the different sensors operate at very
different native \emph{sampling rates}: the vibration channels produce, by orders of
magnitude, the most samples per cycle, while temperature is sampled far more slowly. This
heterogeneity means that the signals cannot be compared sample by sample without an
explicit alignment strategy, and it is the reason the existing pipeline deliberately keeps
every signal at its \emph{native} rate rather than \emph{resampling} or interpolating all
signals onto a common grid (Section~\ref{sec:Grundlagen_Pipeline}). Preserving the native
rate avoids introducing interpolation artefacts, but it also makes the number of samples
per signal an informative quality indicator in its own right.

\subsection{Cycle and Event Detection}
\label{subsec:Grundlagen_TS_Zyklen}

Extracting individual motion cycles from a continuous recording is an instance of
\emph{time-series segmentation}: partitioning a signal into meaningful, internally
homogeneous intervals \parencite{TODO-time-series-segmentation}. The simplest and most
transparent segmentation approach is \emph{threshold-based}: an event boundary is placed
wherever the signal crosses a fixed level. This is the approach used for cycle detection
here, where a cycle is delimited by the position signal crossing a \emph{motion threshold}
upward (cycle start) and downward again (cycle end). Threshold-based detection is easy to
justify and to reproduce, but its result depends critically on the chosen threshold, which
must be separated from sensor noise around the rest position.

More general alternatives exist. \emph{Change-point detection} methods identify points at
which the statistical properties of a signal change, without requiring a predefined
level \parencite{TODO-changepoint-detection}; model-based segmentation fits an explicit
signal model and marks deviations from it. These methods are more flexible than a fixed
threshold but also more complex and harder to explain to a domain expert. Because the
actuator is position-controlled and follows a well-defined trajectory, a threshold on the
position signal is sufficient and preferable for delimiting cycles; the more general
methods are noted here as the conceptual background against which that design choice is
made.

\subsection{Standstill and Frozen-Signal Detection}
\label{subsec:Grundlagen_TS_Stillstand}

Detecting that a signal is \emph{not} moving is a separate problem from detecting cycle
boundaries. A \emph{standstill} within a supposedly active cycle -- caused for example by a
\emph{frozen} sensor that repeats its last value, or by a communication dropout -- can
otherwise pass undetected, because a constant value above the motion threshold still looks
like an ``active'' cycle. The general idea for detecting standstill is to examine, within a
sliding time window, whether the signal actually varies: a signal whose values do not
change by more than a small amount over a window is considered to be at
standstill \parencite{TODO-steady-state-detection}. Two indicators are available for this
in the present data. The position signal must change by more than a small minimum amount
within each window; alternatively -- and physically more directly -- the magnitude of the
\emph{velocity} signal must differ from zero by more than a small threshold, since a
velocity near zero is a more direct indication of true standstill than a small position
change. The velocity-based variant is methodologically preferable but presupposes that the
physical scaling of the velocity signal has been verified; this dependency is taken up in
the methodology chapter.

\subsection{Robust Thresholds from Data Distributions}
\label{subsec:Grundlagen_TS_Schwellwerte}

A recurring requirement in this work is to derive a numerical threshold -- an acceptable
range for a cycle duration, a sample count, or a coverage ratio -- from observed data
rather than to set it by hand. The difficulty is that observed distributions contain
outliers, and estimators such as the arithmetic mean and the standard deviation are
themselves strongly influenced by exactly those outliers. \emph{Robust} statistics avoid
this by using estimators that are insensitive to a fraction of extreme values. Two robust
approaches are relevant here. \emph{Quantile-} or \emph{percentile-based} limits define
the acceptable range directly through, for example, the 1st and 99th percentile of the
observed values, so that a fixed fraction of extreme values falls outside the range by
construction; a closely related idea is the interquartile-range (IQR) ``fence'' rule for
flagging outliers \parencite{TODO-tukey-eda}. Alternatively, the \emph{median absolute
deviation} (MAD) provides a robust measure of spread around the median, and a threshold can
be placed at a fixed number of MAD units from the median \parencite{TODO-robust-statistics-mad}.
Both approaches yield thresholds that are reproducible and traceable to the data, and both
underpin the data-driven criteria developed in Chapter~\ref{kap:Methodik}. Whichever is
used, a threshold learned from a population that mixes healthy and degraded cycles
describes the spread of the observed data, not a guaranteed boundary of \emph{usable} data
-- a limitation that is discussed where these methods are applied.

\section{The Existing Preprocessing Pipeline}
\label{sec:Grundlagen_Pipeline}

The raw recordings do not reach this work as loose measurements but as structured motion
cycles, produced by a preprocessing pipeline that originates from a parallel master's
thesis in the same corporate environment \parencite{TODO-heydari-2026}. This pipeline is
\emph{not} part of the present work; it is its starting basis and is summarised here as
prior work so that the contribution of this thesis (Chapter~\ref{kap:Methodik}) can be
located precisely against it. The pipeline is organised as a sequence of eleven stages, of
which the first nine are implemented and the last two are planned:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Metadata integration} -- catalogues which raw signals exist and with which
        properties, and reconstructs the hierarchical signal information.
  \item \textbf{Signal discovery} -- selects the measurement channels belonging to a given
        experiment and identifies the position channel used as the cycle reference.
  \item \textbf{Timestamp analysis} -- computes the sampling-interval and gap statistics of
        the reference signal, providing the basis for later threshold decisions.
  \item \textbf{Recording-session detection} -- splits the continuous recording into
        independent \emph{recording sessions} wherever the time gap between two samples
        exceeds a configured limit, so that cycle detection never spans a rig interruption.
  \item \textbf{Position-based cycle detection} -- derives individual motion cycles from
        the position signal via the motion threshold, discarding incomplete boundary
        cycles; detection is performed separately within each session.
  \item \textbf{Multi-sensor cycle extraction} -- extracts every configured sensor signal
        within each cycle's time window, each at its native sampling rate, without
        resampling or interpolation.
  \item \textbf{Cycle quality profiling} -- computes purely descriptive quality metrics per
        cycle and per signal (sample counts, sampling rate, maximum time gap, coverage
        ratio, signal presence, and range); this stage deliberately makes \emph{no}
        keep/reject decision.
  \item \textbf{Validation rule generation} -- derives statistical validation thresholds
        from the profiled quality distributions, primarily using a median-plus-MAD rule
        with a quantile-based fallback.
  \item \textbf{Dataset validation} -- applies the frozen thresholds to classify each cycle
        as technically valid or invalid and separates the cycles accordingly.
  \item \textbf{Feature engineering} \emph{(planned)} -- transformation of valid cycles into
        machine-learning-ready feature representations.
  \item \textbf{Dataset generation} \emph{(planned)} -- assembly of the final structured
        dataset with training/validation/test partitions.
\end{enumerate}

Two properties of this pipeline matter for the present work. First, to cope with the sheer
size of the recordings -- hundreds of millions of samples per run -- the pipeline reads
data through filtered, partition-aware queries (predicate pushdown) that load only the
requested signal and time window instead of scanning the complete measurement table; this
shared loading infrastructure is what makes the analysis tractable at all. Second, stages~8
and~9 already constitute a \emph{statistical} validation layer that does make a keep/reject
decision. However, as the pipeline's own documentation notes, its central thresholds are
partly provisional (most notably the motion threshold), and its validation rules are
learned from a cycle population that mixes healthy and degraded behaviour, so they
represent technical data-quality boundaries for the observed population rather than
justified boundaries of usable data. These acknowledged limitations, together with the
absence of an explicit standstill check and of any account of how well each criterion is
justified, are the precise gaps that the methodology of the following chapter addresses.
